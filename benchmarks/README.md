# Konjugate benchmarks

Konjugate is evaluated against established numerical and engineering benchmarks, with reproducible reference comparisons, conservation checks and performance measurements. This directory holds those benchmarks: each problem's model, its reference, its configuration and its thresholds, and the runner that measures Konjugate against them and writes a report. Where each problem and each reference comes from, with links, and the licences of the third-party data included here, are under [Where the problems come from](#where-the-problems-come-from).

The benchmarks measure what Konjugate does today, including where it is weak. They are not a claim that Konjugate is accurate for every problem; they say how accurate it is for these, and why.

## What is being measured

By default Konjugate integrates every node with fixed-step Euler: explicit Euler, or semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler for a node whose `numerics.integration` is `semiImplicit` (`engine/src/executionPlan.cpp`, `compileExecutionPlan` and `integrateNode`). A semi-implicit node updates its states one after another in the order it lists them, each from contributions evaluated with the states already updated. Both methods are first order. A node may instead be integrated with Verlet (`numerics.integration: "verlet"`, [Integrators, Verlet, node by node](../docs/proposals/integrators.md#verlet-node-by-node)), Störmer–Verlet, second order and symplectic: its states, in their listed order, fall in two parts, each read only by the other (positions and their speeds), and each substep moves the first part half a step, the second a whole step and the first the other half. A node whose rates do not split so, as when a rate reads its own state, is refused in words. A run configuration may instead choose Runge–Kutta 4 (`method: "rungeKutta4"`, [Integrators](../docs/proposals/integrators.md)), the classical fourth-order method applied to the whole model at once: at each of its four stages every node is evaluated from every node's stage values, so the coupling between nodes is as accurate as the method; or Adaptive (`method: "dormandPrince54"`), Dormand–Prince 5(4) applied the same way, with error control to a relative and absolute tolerance and its steps landing on every global step, or Tsit5 (`method: "tsit5"`), Tsitouras's 5(4) pair on the same scheme and controller, which the app chooses under Adaptive; or Stiff (`method: "rodas4"`), Rodas4, a Rosenbrock method of order four, L-stable, applied the same way, its Jacobian worked out each step exactly from the equations' derivatives (or by finite differences, as it first was, when the run asks) on the structure the model's graph gives and solved with Konjugate's own LU. Rodas5P (`method: "rodas5p"`, [Integrators, Rodas5P](../docs/proposals/integrators.md#rodas5p-stiff-fifth-order)), Steinebach's eight-stage Rosenbrock method of order five, L-stable, works the same way; the app chooses it under Stiff when the relative tolerance is tighter than 1e-6, where it takes half or less of Rodas4's steps. BDF (`method: "bdf"`), the numerical differentiation formulas of orders 1 to 5, keeps that Jacobian and factorisation from step to step, for stiff models of thousands of states.

Under Euler, each global step, every node takes its own substeps (`numerics.substepsPerGlobalStep`) and reads every other node through a snapshot frozen at the start of the global step ([Parallel execution](../docs/parallelExecution.md)). A relationship's bindings to its own target node read that node's live state; bindings to any other node read the snapshot. Coupling between nodes is therefore first order in the global step, whatever the substeps, and a semi-implicit or Verlet node's own scheme applies only to its own states, never across nodes.

The problems were chosen to suit these methods: smooth, non-stiff systems where a first-order method converges and its behaviour can be measured cleanly. Stiff problems (Robertson's chemical kinetics, Van der Pol at μ = 1000, Prothero–Robinson) are run with Stiff, and with Adaptive only over a short stretch beside it, to count the steps an explicit method needs; with fixed-step Euler or Runge–Kutta 4 they would measure only that an explicit method is unsuitable, which is known. The numerical classics (Van der Pol at μ = 1 and 10, Lorenz, the double pendulum) measure every method where it is weak as well as where it is strong: a chaotic trajectory is compared only up to the predictability horizon each method reaches, which is reported, and beyond it the system is judged by what can still be judged, its statistics and its sensitivity; Van der Pol at μ = 1000 is run with Stiff. Lorenz is split over two nodes, x and {y, z}, not three: a relationship reads only the two nodes it connects, and the equations of y and z each read both other variables (x z and x y), so three single-state nodes cannot express them today.

Each problem directory holds:

- `problem.mjs`: the model, built as an ordinary Konjugate project document; the reference; and the evaluation, which runs the model, compares it with the reference and reports rows, checks and plots. The header comment states the equations.
- `benchmark.json`: the runs (global steps, horizons, output intervals), the timing cases for `--full`, and the thresholds every check is judged against.
- The `.kjt` models, written by `node benchmarks/generateModels.mjs` through the same project encoder the app saves with. They open in Konjugate like any other project. The runner refuses to run if a committed model differs from what its `problem.mjs` builds, so a model and its reference cannot drift apart.
- `reference.json`, where the problem has numerical reference data: the data, the method, the tolerances and the versions of Python, NumPy and SciPy that made it.

A run may apply a variant to a committed model before running it, and only three kinds, recorded in every result row: the integration method of every node (`explicit`, `semiImplicit` or `verlet`), the substeps of every node, and the run's method (`rungeKutta4`, `dormandPrince54`, `tsit5`, `rodas4`, `rodas5p` or `bdf`, with its steps per global step, its tolerance and, for Stiff, its `jacobian`, set on the run configuration the engine is given, not on the model). Robertson, Van der Pol at μ = 1000, the stiff network and Prothero–Robinson run Stiff once more with `jacobian: "finiteDifference"` beside the default exact Jacobian, and check the evaluations and error of the one against the other (and, for Prothero–Robinson, the order at the finest step, which finite differences distort). Everything else is the committed model.

### Graph scalability

`scaling/graphScalability` generates its models when it runs (`graphs.mjs` states the equations; no `.kjt` is committed): a chain of n thermal masses with both ends held at ambient, an m × m grid with its edges held at ambient, and a random sparse network (a ring plus n random paths, seeded, random conductances and capacities, no ambient). `node benchmarks/scaling/graphScalability/graphs.mjs <chain | grid | random> <nodes> <output.kjt>` writes one to open in Konjugate. The chain and grid start as a sum of three of their sine modes, so their exact motion, and each fixed-step method's exact result (a mode grows by 1 + hλ a step under Euler, by the fourth-order polynomial in hλ under Runge–Kutta 4), is known at every node at any size: Konjugate's numbers are checked against the method's exact steps (they agree to about 1e-14) and against the exact solution (the method's error). The random network is checked against the method stepped independently in the benchmark, by the matrix exponential at 100 nodes, and by its conserved total heat. Every graph runs on all three backends with the workers and partitions fixed (4 each), so the accuracy run's results do not depend on the machine; the backends must agree bit for bit. Methods are entries in its `benchmark.json`, so Dormand–Prince and Rodas4 can be added as variants: a method with no exact steps to compare with is judged against the exact solution only.

Under `--full`, the problem's `measure()` times the same families at 100, 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 nodes (each case about a million node steps, one warm-up and five timed runs), on every backend under every method, and sweeps the worker count from 1 to 8 on a 10,000-node grid. It reports the engine process's wall time split into compiling and planning (the engine's `planningNanoseconds`), solving (its synchronization compute time) and the rest (starting, reading and validating the project, writing the result), node steps per second of solving, peak resident memory, and each parallel backend's speed-up over serial and efficiency per worker. A million nodes is not measured: the project would be about 1.45 GB of JSON, more than the project encoder can hold in one string, and the engine's validation alone would take minutes; the report says so.

### Causal reconstruction

`causal/reconstruction` measures Konjugate's causal inference (`konjugateEngine infer`, [Causal inference](../docs/causalInference.md)) on datasets whose truth is known. `datasets.mjs` generates each one from a seed: a discrete structural model stepped once per row (lag-1 linear terms, a self-lag, powers of a source, a source-target product, and, deliberately outside the model `infer` fits, a delay of two rows, an effect within one row, a hidden variable and a near-noiseless copy), or a continuous-time linear system integrated by Euler–Maruyama and sampled every 0.1, 0.5, 1 or 2 s. The CSVs and their truths are committed in `data/` (about 1.1 MB); the runner regenerates each in memory and refuses to run if a committed CSV differs. For each, the graph is hidden and `infer` runs on the CSV alone, with its default settings except the polynomial degrees or interaction terms a dataset calls for. A proposed edge is one with a lagged fit and a direction (`continuousLagged`); precision, recall and F1 are over those, a skeleton F1 also counts the `correlationOnly` pairs, direction is the share of found true edges reported in exactly the right direction, and the equation error is ‖estimated − true rates‖ / ‖true rates‖ over every true or proposed edge, in the engine's own units (rates per second of the CSV's time column). Datasets inside the model class have a floor on F1 just below its observed value; datasets showing a known limit have a band on both sides of the measure that shows it, and each says why it fails.

### Engineering systems

The problems above measure the integrators, the engine and causal inference on model problems. The engineering problems ask a different question: whether a model built the way an engineer builds one in Konjugate, as a graph of components joined by relationships, reproduces an independent reference for an interconnected system, conserves what it should, and where it falls short. They are lumped-parameter models: each node is a component or a well-mixed volume, each relationship a flow, force or signal between two of them. Spatial problems, finite-element structural analysis and computational fluid dynamics such as the NAFEMS benchmarks, are out of scope: Konjugate does not discretise partial differential equations in space, and a graph of segments (as in the heat exchanger) is the modeller's own discretisation, measured as such.

Unlike the problems above, these are not all smooth: switches, diodes, valves, clamps and step inputs are part of engineering models. Runge–Kutta 4, Adaptive and Stiff locate a switch written as a condition in an equation (docs/proposals/integrators.md, Event location) and step to it exactly; explicit Euler, node by node, does not. Each problem compares explicit Euler and Runge–Kutta 4 (and semi-implicit Euler where it matters), each at a step chosen for the problem and over a range of steps for the observed order, with the method set on the run as a variant, so a method added later (Dormand–Prince, Rodas4; [Integrators](../docs/proposals/integrators.md)) is another entry in each problem's list of methods and steps.

Each problem keeps its reference generator beside it (`generateReference.py`), with its own copy of the constants and equations, and its `reference.json` records the method, tolerances and the versions of every tool that made it. The tools are installed outside the repository and never committed:

- SciPy (`solve_ivp` DOP853 and Radau, `expm`), in the virtual environment above.
- ngspice (47, `brew install ngspice` or the platform's package) for the switching circuits.
- FMPy (0.3.32 or later, which carries CVODE for Apple Silicon) for the Reference FMUs, in its own environment: `uv venv -p 3.12 /tmp/konjugate-fmpy-venv`, then `VIRTUAL_ENV=/tmp/konjugate-fmpy-venv uv pip install fmpy numpy`.
- The Modelica Association's published MSL reference results, which `modelica/standardLibrary/generateReference.py` downloads from github.com/modelica/MAP-LIB_ReferenceResults (branch v4.1.0) and thins, recording each file's SHA-256. They are part of the Modelica Standard Library (BSD 3-Clause).
- The Reference FMUs (v0.0.42, 2-Clause BSD), committed unchanged in `fmi/referenceFmus/fmus/` with their licence; the benchmark checks their SHA-256. They ship binaries for Apple Silicon Macs, Linux (x86_64 and aarch64) and Windows (x86_64 and x86), none for an Intel Mac. The benchmark imports those with a binary for the computer it runs on, decided as the app decides it when it installs an FMU, and requires every one of them to import and run as published; the others it reports as having no binary for that computer, not as a failure, so on an Intel Mac none is imported. A check also requires the app's choice to agree with the binaries/ directory the FMU ships for that computer, so an importer that stops finding a binary is not taken for a missing one. `KONJUGATE_BENCHMARK_FMU_PLATFORM=darwin-x64` (Node's platform and processor names) shows what it reports on another computer.

What these problems show Konjugate does well: graph models of up to 26 nodes reproduce independent references to their integrators' accuracy; every balance that is linear in the states (water volume, the thermal energy of the motor and the data centre, the heat exchanger's enthalpy) is kept to rounding under both methods; Runge–Kutta 4 is fourth order on every smooth problem and, with its switches located, on every switched one but the data centre's clamps: the heat exchanger, the fluid network and all four Modelica examples among them; the discretised heat exchanger reaches its exact equilibrium to rounding, its distance from ε-NTU being the segmentation's, falling as 1/N; and the four FMI 3.0 Reference FMUs import as published and give FMPy's co-simulation of them to the bit (the difference from their model exchange is the FMUs' own fixed-step solvers, largest for the bouncing ball).

What they show it does not do, plainly:

- **Explicit Euler does not locate switches.** Under it a switch, a diode or a clamp changes at the first step that starts after the instant it should, so the switched buck converter and the rectifier converge at first order. Runge–Kutta 4, Adaptive and Stiff locate every switch written as a condition (a comparison inside a piecewise equation's condition) and step to it, so Runge–Kutta 4 is fourth order on them again: from 1.21 to 3.96 on the switched buck, 1.99 to 4.12 on the rectifier, 1.00 to 4.28 on the DC motor's step inputs and 1.00 to 4.00 on the Cauer filter's, each with its error falling by four to eight orders of magnitude at the same step.
- **Kinks are not located.** A `Min`, `Max` or `Abs` of a quantity that changes sign is continuous but not smooth, and costs an order where it turns: the data centre's cooling units clamp their demand at zero and at capacity, which keeps Runge–Kutta 4 at about 3.4 there (its time switches, the batch job and the coil failure, are located).
- **No acausal network solving or index reduction.** The Cauer filter's capacitance matrix and the drive train's rigid gear were reduced by hand to explicit equations, as a Modelica tool does automatically; the equations are the same, but the work is the modeller's.
- **Explicit Euler on undamped resonance.** The Modelica `Oscillator`, driven at resonance, is 9 % of its range off under explicit Euler at 16,000 steps per second; semi-implicit Euler, 0.3 %.
- **FMI 3 aliases are not ports.** An FMI 3 `<Alias>` (BouncingBall's `h_ft`, its height in feet) is not offered as a port of its own; the variable it names is, and is compared.

## References

Analytic references are preferred, and every problem but Lotka–Volterra and the numerical classics (Van der Pol, Lorenz, the double pendulum) has one. Linear systems (the thermal network, the RLC circuits, the coupled masses) are solved exactly with the matrix exponential of the augmented system [[A, b], [0, 0]] (`lib/linearAlgebra.mjs`, Higham's scaling and squaring with a degree 13 Padé approximant), solved about an offset near the solution (the ambient temperature) so that the exponential stays well scaled. The pendulum's exact motion is in Jacobi elliptic functions, computed by the arithmetic-geometric mean (`lib/elliptic.mjs`). The energy an RLC circuit dissipates follows exactly from its energy balance: the source's work less the energy stored.

The analytic references are themselves checked: `reference/generateReferences.py` computes each one again, independently, in Python with SciPy (`scipy.linalg.expm`, `scipy.special.ellipj`), from its own copy of the constants and equations, and each benchmark run reports how closely the two agree (relative to each quantity's magnitude, currently between 3e-15 and 1e-12).

Where there is no closed form, the reference is a numerical solution by an independent high-accuracy solver: SciPy's `solve_ivp` with the 8th-order Dormand–Prince method (DOP853) at rtol = atol = 1e-12. A numerical reference is never treated as absolute truth. Its own error is recorded where it can be measured (the drift of Lotka–Volterra's conserved quantity in the reference is about 1e-10 of the orbit's size), and it is used only where that error is far below the errors being measured. The pendulum is also solved with DOP853, as a check of the method on a problem whose answer is known.

The numerical classics have no closed form, and a chaotic reference has a further difficulty: an error in the reference grows like any other, e^(λt), so a reference is only as good as the time over which its own error stays far below what is measured. Each such problem has its own generator beside it (`generateReference.py`) and records its own error with its data. Lorenz is solved by a Taylor series method in 70-digit arithmetic, checked against itself at a lower order and precision (they agree to 6e-30 over 40 s); SciPy's DOP853 at rtol = atol = 1e-12 is solved too, and loses the trajectory (1 % of a state's range) at 33.6 s, which shows why a double-precision reference was not enough. The double pendulum is solved by DOP853 at rtol = atol = 1e-13, its own error estimated at every sample against 1e-12, and every comparison stops where that estimate reaches 1 % of the angle the comparison is judged at (17.0 s). Van der Pol is solved by Radau with the exact Jacobian at rtol = atol = 1e-12, checked against DOP853 (they agree to 4e-12 at μ = 1 and 7e-11 at μ = 10); the limit cycle's period at μ = 1, 6.663286859323169 s, is within 6e-15 of the published 6.663286859323130 s. Statistics of the Lorenz attractor are not a trajectory and need no tight trajectory: they come from 200 runs started on the attractor, each averaged over a window as long as the benchmark's own run, so the scatter across them is the scatter one run should show; their mean Lyapunov exponent, 0.9059 /s, agrees with Viswanath's 0.9056 within its standard error.

To regenerate the reference data, in a virtual environment outside the repository (the environment is not committed; the data and the scripts are):

```bash
python3 -m venv /tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/pip install numpy scipy mpmath
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/reference/generateReferences.py
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/oscillators/vanDerPol/generateReference.py
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/chaos/lorenz/generateReference.py
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/mechanical/doublePendulum/generateReference.py
```

The Lorenz generator takes a few minutes (the 70-digit Taylor series) and Van der Pol's several (Radau at μ = 1000); every reference.json records the versions of Python, NumPy, SciPy and, for Lorenz, mpmath that made it.

## Metrics

Every comparison is made at the run's output samples, which are the same for every step size in a study.

- **Max abs error**: the largest |Konjugate - reference| over every compared state and sample, in the states' own units.
- **RMSE**: the root mean square of the same differences.
- **Relative error**: for each state, its largest absolute error divided by the reference's range over the run (max - min); the largest over the states. The range is used rather than the value, so a temperature near 300 K or a current passing through zero is judged against how much it actually moves.
- **Conservation error**: the problem's conserved or balanced quantity, from Konjugate's own states. For the thermal network, the energy balance Σ C ΔT against heat supplied less heat lost, relative to heat supplied. For the RLC circuits, the source's work V q against energy stored plus energy dissipated, relative to the source's work. For the oscillator and pendulum, the largest drift of the energy relative to its initial value (for the pendulum, relative to the energy above the bottom of the swing, E(0) + g L). For Lotka–Volterra, the largest drift of V = δx - γ ln x + βy - α ln y relative to V(0) - V*, the orbit's level above the equilibrium.
- **Steady-state error**: the largest difference between the final state of a long run and the exact equilibrium A x* = -b.
- **Observed order**: p in error ≈ C hᵖ, from each halving of the step (log₂ of the error ratio) and as the least-squares slope of log error against log h. Euler's methods should show p ≈ 1, Verlet p ≈ 2 (judged over Euler's step sizes, on the oscillator and the pendulum only: in the thermal network and the RLC circuits a state's rate reads that state itself, a loss, which Verlet refuses), Runge–Kutta 4 p ≈ 4, Dormand–Prince 5(4) with its error control off p ≈ 5 (a run configuration turns it off by giving it `stepsPerGlobalStep`). Runge–Kutta 4 is judged over step sizes of its own (`rungeKutta4GlobalSteps` in `verification/convergenceOrder/benchmark.json`), chosen where its error is well above rounding and its step inside its stability region: at Euler's steps its error would reach rounding within a halving or two. Dormand–Prince 5(4) likewise (`dormandPrince54GlobalSteps`); it is not judged on the thermal network, whose error passes from steps too long to show the order straight to the reference's rounding.
- **Tolerance proportionality** (Adaptive): over relative tolerances from 1e-3 to 1e-10, each absolute tolerance a thousandth of it, with a global step a sixteenth of the run so the global step does not bind, the least-squares slope of log error against log tolerance, and the largest error over the tolerance. Dormand–Prince's slope is a little under 1, its error estimate being of order four. Each run's work (steps taken and rejected, evaluations of the model) is in its row, and each problem has a work–precision plot of every method's contributions evaluated against the error it reached.
- **Coupling error**: for the split model, its difference from the one-node model run at the same effective step (the global step divided by the substeps), relative to range.
- **Predictability horizon**: for a chaotic problem, the first sample time at which the error exceeds a stated tolerance (for Lorenz 1 % of a state's range, for the double pendulum 0.1 rad in either angle). An error grows like e^(λt), so halving the step of a method of order p gains only about p ln 2 / λ seconds of horizon.
- **Statistical score**: for a long-run property of the Lorenz attractor (the mean and standard deviation of z, the root mean square of x, the largest Lyapunov exponent), |Konjugate - reference| in units of the scatter of one run as long as Konjugate's across the reference ensemble. One run cannot be expected to land closer than about 1 to 2; a method whose score stays well above that has moved the attractor.
- **Limit cycle**: the Van der Pol cycle's period, from upward zero crossings of x, and its amplitude, the largest x, both found between samples by cubic Hermite interpolation with the exact slopes, after a long transient, relative to the reference's.
- **Work**: the global steps, the node substeps executed and the contributions evaluated, summed over nodes, as the engine reports them (`execution.nodeMetrics`).

## Thresholds

Every check is judged against a threshold in its problem's `benchmark.json`, so a change in Konjugate's numbers shows up as a failing check rather than a silent difference. Accuracy is deterministic: the same engine on the same platform gives the same numbers, and across platforms they differ only by rounding. The thresholds are set as follows:

- An error at a configured step has a ceiling about 25 % above its observed value: a regression guard, not a claim that the error is small.
- A known weakness of explicit Euler (the growth of an undamped oscillator's energy, the drift of Lotka–Volterra's conserved quantity) has a band on both sides of its observed value, so a change to the integrator that alters it in either direction is noticed and the benchmark updated knowingly.
- Quantities that should be at rounding level (an energy balance that explicit Euler keeps exactly, a steady state it reaches exactly, the agreement of two exact references) have absolute limits a few orders above rounding.
- Identities (a split model with one substep against one node, semi-implicit against explicit integration for one-state nodes, and under Runge–Kutta 4 the split model against one node at any step, under Adaptive at any tolerance) must hold exactly, or to 1e-12.
- A predictability horizon, or a statistical score of a method known to move the attractor, has a band on both sides of its observed value; a score of Runge–Kutta 4 at a step where it does not move the attractor must be at most 3.
- Euler's observed orders must lie within 0.95 to 1.10 (fitted over all six steps) and 0.97 to 1.03 (from the finest halving); Verlet's within 1.9 to 2.1, Runge–Kutta 4's within 3.9 to 4.1 and Dormand–Prince 5(4)'s at fixed steps within 4.9 to 5.1, both fitted and from the finest halving. Adaptive's slope of error against tolerance must lie within 0.8 to 1.15, and its error within 20 times its relative tolerance.

When a deliberate change to the engine moves a result, the threshold is updated with the change, and the reason recorded in its commit.

## Running

```bash
npm run benchmark:accuracy   # builds the engine, then: node benchmarks/run.mjs --accuracy
npm run benchmark:full       # builds the engine, then: node benchmarks/run.mjs --full
npm run benchmark:models     # writes the .kjt models from the problems
```

`--accuracy` runs every problem, deterministically and with no timing, and exits with status 1 if any check fails. It takes about forty seconds and writes to `out/benchmarks/accuracy/`. `--full` runs the same accuracy checks and then the timing cases, and writes to `benchmarks/results/<machine>/`. Other options: `--engine <path>` (or `KONJUGATE_ENGINE`) to use a particular engine build, `--output <dir>`, `--only <text>` to run the problems whose ID contains the text, and for `--full`, `--repeats <n>` (default 7), `--warmup <n>` (default 1) and `--no-caffeinate`.

Each run writes:

- `report.html`: one self-contained page (no network, no external files) with every problem's plots of Konjugate against its reference, its checks and its result rows, and for `--full` the timings and the machine. Each plot has a data table, and its full data is in `plots/`.
- `results.csv`: every result row, with the columns described under Metrics.
- `checks.csv`: every check, its value, its threshold and whether it passed.
- `summary.json`: the run (versions, commit, mode), the totals, every problem's rows and checks, and for `--full` the machine and timings.
- `plots/<id>.svg` and `plots/<id>.csv`: each plot on its own, and its data.
- `timing.csv` (`--full` only): each timing case's minimum, median and spread.
- `timing-conditions.csv` (`--full --wait-for-quiet`): every timed run's instant, label, one-minute load average and whether Docker was running.

## Timing

Timing is measured only by `--full`, and only means anything on the machine that measured it. It depends on the processor, its power state and thermal state, the operating system's scheduling and whatever else is running, none of which travel with the code. So `--full` records the machine with its results (model, processor and core counts, memory, operating system, power source, battery charge and Low Power Mode where the platform reports them, and the load average at the start; never its host name, user or serial number), and its results are kept per machine under `benchmarks/results/`, each part of each session recording the Konjugate version, the commit it was built from and whether the checkout had uncommitted changes. Comparing timings across machines, or across versions measured on different machines, compares the machines as much as Konjugate.

Every timing's headline is its minimum, for every tool and in every table and plot, with its median, interquartile range and the minimum over the median beside it. The reason: on a computer, everything that is not the program being timed (another process, an interrupt, a cache another process emptied, a slower clock) only ever adds time, so the minimum of repeated runs is the measurement least disturbed by the computer and the most repeatable from one session to another, as BenchmarkTools.jl reports it for the same reason; the median and the spread beside it say how much disturbance there was. Across sessions the headline is the smallest of the sessions' minimums, with the spread between them, (largest − smallest) / smallest, and a measurement is flagged as noisy when a session's interquartile range exceeds 5 % of its median. For each timing case, the runner makes untimed warm-up runs, then the timed runs one at a time, and reports the minimum wall time with its median, maximum, median absolute deviation and interquartile range, the median of the engine's own integration time (`execution.synchronizationComputeNanoseconds`), the node substeps integrated per second, and the engine process's peak resident memory (from `/usr/bin/time` on macOS and Linux). Wall time covers the whole engine process: starting, reading the model, integrating and writing the result.

Timing runs need a quiet machine. Before timing, the runner looks for Konjugate's interaction test runners, Konjugate's own Electron (the development build or the installed app) and test-driven Chrome, and warns if any is running, as it does when the computer is on battery or in Low Power Mode; the warning is kept in the report. On macOS it runs under `caffeinate -ims`, so the computer does not sleep or idle-throttle during the run. With `--wait-for-quiet` (`lib/quiet.mjs`) it also waits before timing until the computer is on mains power, no compiler, build tool, Konjugate Electron or interaction test is running, and the one-minute load average has stayed below 2 for two minutes; and before every timed run it checks again that it is on mains power, that none of those has started and that other processes use less than two cores (the load average then counts the benchmark's own runs), waiting for the full gate again if not. The report says how often it waited. `--full` records the engine's build with the machine: its CMake build type, compiler, the flags its sources were actually compiled with (from `compile_commands.json`) and whether it is the build the app ships. The published results time the engine the app ships, the `development` preset's RelWithDebInfo build (`-O2 -g -DNDEBUG`) in `out/engine`, as `npm run build:engine` builds it, CI packages it and the accuracy checks run it, so one engine is checked, timed and shipped; no separately optimised build is timed.

## Reproduce these results

Everything here can be reproduced from a clone, with nothing installed by hand and no administrator rights. macOS (Apple silicon or Intel) and Linux (x64 or arm64) are supported; the performance scripts do not support Windows (the accuracy benchmarks, `npm run benchmark:accuracy`, do run there).

```bash
git clone https://github.com/zenineasa/konjugate.git && cd konjugate
npm install
npm run setup                      # once: the engine's development dependencies (vcpkg)
npm run benchmark:setup            # the shipped engine (out/engine) and the pinned comparison tools, downloaded and checked
npm run benchmark:performance      # a timing session: problems, scaling and comparisons, about 4 h 40 min on an Apple M1
npm run benchmark:openmodelica     # optional, afterwards, with Docker running: OpenModelica, not timing-comparable
```

**Setup.** `npm run benchmark:setup` (`benchmarks/setup.mjs`) builds the engine the app ships, the `development` preset's RelWithDebInfo build (`-O2`) in `out/engine`, as `npm run build:engine` builds it (only `konjugateEngine`, not the engine's tests; nothing when it is current; a directory configured otherwise is configured again), compiling at most one file per processor and per 2 GiB of memory at once, four on an 8 GB computer, or `CMAKE_BUILD_PARALLEL_LEVEL` when set, with vcpkg held to the same; and it installs, into `KONJUGATE_BENCHMARK_TOOLS` (by default `~/.konjugate-benchmarks`), at the versions pinned in `comparisons/tools.mjs`: uv, downloaded and checked against its SHA-256; through it a standalone Python and a virtual environment with exactly the packages of `comparisons/requirements.lock` (compiled from `comparisons/requirements.in`), each checked against its hash; and the official Julia build, checked against its SHA-256, with exactly the packages of `comparisons/julia/Manifest.toml` instantiated in its own depot and precompiled. When Docker is running it also pulls OpenModelica's image, pinned by digest; without Docker it says so and goes on. It refuses to build the engine while its sources or the harness have uncommitted changes, naming them (below; `--allow-uncommitted` builds it regardless). Run again, it checks every piece and repairs what is missing or not as pinned; `npm run benchmark:setup -- --check` only reports what is installed and its versions, for the engine its build type, optimisation, compiler and flags, or that it is not the shipped build. The first setup is a one-time cost: on an Apple M1 (8 GB) it downloaded about 0.4 GB (Julia 213 MiB, uv 14 MiB, Python 24 MiB, NumPy and SciPy, and Julia's packages), took 1.6 GB of disk (Julia 781 MB, its packages and their precompiled code 614 MB, Python and its packages 165 MB, uv and its cache 124 MB) and 24 minutes, of which about 5 were precompiling Julia's packages and much of the rest a slow download; later runs check it in seconds. Building the engine comes on top, with `npm run setup`'s dependencies, unless `npm run build:engine` has already built it.

**Before a timing session**, on the computer that will publish the numbers:

- commit every change first: a session refuses to start while anything the timed engine or the benchmark harness is built from has uncommitted changes (below);
- plug in the power, and leave it plugged in;
- quit Docker Desktop and every other application: editors, browsers, chat, Agent Orchestrator, the Konjugate app; let builds and tests finish;
- run the command from a terminal (Terminal or iTerm, not an editor's), and leave the computer alone until it says Done; it keeps itself awake.

**A session.** `npm run benchmark:performance` (`benchmarks/performance.mjs`) brings the shipped engine and the tools up to date, then runs three parts, printing how long each should take, its progress and the time left:

| Part | What it times | About, on an Apple M1 |
|---|---|---|
| `problems` | every problem's timing cases (`run.mjs --full --no-scaling`): one warm-up, seven timed runs each, after the accuracy checks | 20 min |
| `scaling` | graph scalability (`run.mjs --full --only scaling/graphScalability`): chains, grids and random networks of 100 to 100,000 nodes on three backends under Euler and Runge–Kutta 4, one warm-up and five timed runs each | 1 h 50 min |
| `comparisons` | Konjugate, its exported C++, SciPy and Julia on seven problems at eight tolerances, five interleaved rounds (below) | 2 h 30 min |

Before each part it refuses to go on, saying why, unless the computer is on mains power, Docker is not running, and no compiler, build tool, Electron application (Agent Orchestrator, an editor, a chat app), Konjugate app or test runner is running. An application counts as running while its own executable is; the crash reporter (`chrome_crashpad_handler`) an Electron application leaves behind is ignored, and any other process one leaves behind after it has quit is named by its path and process number, to be quit by hand. It then measures the idle computer for three minutes (`lib/quiet.mjs --baseline`): the median one-minute load average and the median processor time of every other process. Every timed run of the part waits until the load average and the other processes' time are below that baseline plus 1 (the full gate holding for two minutes after any wait; a run that waited is timed as usual once it is quiet, not counted as a failure), and is logged with its instant, load and whether Docker was running (`timing-conditions*.csv`); the baseline and limits are kept with the results. `--only problems,scaling` (any of the three) runs only those parts, so a session need not take four hours; `--dry-run` checks everything in about half an hour, untimed (one repetition, graphs up to 1,000 nodes, one round of the comparisons without the largest grid, no waiting), into `out/benchmarks/performance-dry-run/`, whose numbers are not for publishing.

**Uncommitted changes.** A session records the commit it timed, so it refuses to time anything that is not a commit. Before it builds the engine, and again before each part, `npm run benchmark:performance` checks the sources the timed engine and the harness are built from (`lib/sourceGuard.mjs`): `engine/`, `protocol/`, `vcpkg.json`, `vcpkgOverlays/`, the engine's build scripts (`scripts/buildEngine.mjs`, `scripts/generateReportProtocol.mjs`), `package.json`, `benchmarks/` except `benchmarks/results/`, and every module outside `benchmarks/` that the harness imports, followed from import to import (`src/engineProtocol.mjs`, `src/projectFile.mjs` and theirs). If any of them has uncommitted changes, or is new and untracked, it refuses to start, names the files and says to commit them first; outside a git checkout, where it cannot tell, it refuses too. `npm run benchmark:setup` refuses to build the engine in the same case (`--skip-engine` sets up only the tools). `--allow-uncommitted`, given to either, goes on regardless: the session is then marked as timed with uncommitted changes in its results (`workingTreeChanges`, and `uncommittedSources` listing the files), and the combined report says so in a notice at its top, since its numbers cannot be traced to a commit. A `--dry-run` only says what is uncommitted. Changes elsewhere, to documentation say, are recorded (`workingTreeChanges`) but do not stop a session.

**Provenance notes.** When what a session recorded is not the whole story, a note may be added beside it afterwards, `sessions/<name>/provenance.json`, without touching its results: for a session timed with uncommitted changes, the commit they were built on (`builtFrom`), the commit they were later committed as (`uncommittedChangesCommittedAs`), each a full hash, and what was established and what was not (`established`, `notEstablished`). The report then shows both commits, linked, in place of "dirty", with a notice at its top and a link to the note. A note never changes which sessions are combined, and one about another commit than its session recorded is refused.

**Sessions.** Each session writes to a folder of its own, `benchmarks/results/<machine>/sessions/<date and time>/<part>/`, and at its end the sessions so far are combined (`benchmarks/combine.mjs`, also `npm run benchmark:combine`) into `benchmarks/results/<machine>/`: `report.html` (the sessions and their conditions, the problem timings, graph scalability and the Performance section with the work–precision diagrams), `combined.json`, `timings.csv` and `comparisons/`. The parts can be run in different sessions, on different days, and repeating a session shows how much the computer's own variation moves the numbers. Only the sessions timed from the newest source are combined: the commit of the latest part that timed Konjugate (a part that recorded no commit counts only if its session started after that commit was made). Sessions timed from an older commit stay in `sessions/` for the record and are listed in the report as superseded, never mixed into its numbers, so timing a changed engine starts the combined numbers afresh without moving anything. The results are not kept per version, since the version changes only at a release. `node benchmarks/combine.mjs --sessions <name,name>` combines a choice of them, by the same rule.

**Publishing.** The reports are published with the web edition, at [web.konjugate.com/benchmarks/](https://web.konjugate.com/benchmarks/), with each release: `.github/workflows/webEdition.yml` writes them with `node benchmarks/pages.mjs <directory>` into `benchmarks/` beside the built app, an index of each computer's combined report and its sessions and, from `benchmarks/results/`, only the files those pages link to (about 14 MiB, 3.6 MiB compressed, for the documented machine, most of it the latest session's problems report, which holds every accuracy plot). A superseded session's report larger than 1 MiB is linked in the repository instead. Every link is relative and checked; a missing file stops the build. The app does not load them: they are added after the build, so they are not in its offline copy, and its service worker leaves `benchmarks/` to the network. `.github/workflows/benchmarkReports.yml` builds and checks the same site on every push of new results, keeping it with the run for 30 days, without deploying: this repository's GitHub Pages site is the web edition, which a deploy of the reports alone would replace.

**OpenModelica**, afterwards and on its own: start Docker, run `npm run benchmark:openmodelica` (`benchmarks/openModelica.mjs`, three rounds, about 30 minutes), then quit Docker Desktop; in a terminal it offers to quit it for you. Docker Desktop may start other containers of yours by itself when it starts (those with a restart policy); the command names them and never touches them, but they run beside OpenModelica, one more reason its times are not comparable. It writes a session of its own and combines again; its errors are compared with the other tools', and its times are labelled "Linux container, background services running, not timing-comparable".

## Comparisons with established tools

Konjugate is evaluated against established tools, reproducibly: the same equations, the same absolute tolerances and the same output samples under Konjugate's engine, its exported C++ (code export, its compiled path), SciPy's `solve_ivp`, Julia's DifferentialEquations.jl and, apart, OpenModelica, at relative tolerances from 1e-3 to 1e-10. The result is a work–precision diagram per problem and family of methods: the error each run reached against the time it took to solve. It is a comparison of methods and implementations on these problems on one computer, not a ranking of tools.

The problems (`comparisons/problems.mjs`) are the suite's own models: Robertson's kinetics to 1e11 s (reference: SciPy's Radau at rtol 1e-12, restarted at every sample, checked against BDF and the committed checkpoints), Van der Pol at μ = 1000 over two periods and Lorenz over 10 s (the committed references), the thermal RC network, the stiff thermal grid at 256 and 1,024 nodes (exact solutions) and the switched buck converter over 156 periods (exact, interval by interval). Each tool is given the equations written out again from the problem's parameters (`scipyRunner.py`, `julia/juliaRunner.jl`, `openmodelica/openmodelica.mjs`), each state's absolute tolerance the relative tolerance times the problem's scale for it (for Robertson the suite's own 1e-12 and 1e-18 at 1e-8), and is asked for the same samples: Konjugate steps to each (its global step is the sample interval), SciPy and Julia interpolate (`t_eval`, `saveat`), OpenModelica uses its output grid. The error is the suite's relative error, the largest difference from the reference over the samples relative to each state's range.

Methods are matched by kind: Konjugate's Adaptive (Dormand–Prince 5(4) and Tsit5) beside SciPy's RK45 and DOP853 and Julia's Tsit5 and DP5; Stiff (Rodas4 and Rodas5P) beside SciPy's Radau and Julia's Rodas4 and Rodas5P; BDF beside SciPy's BDF and LSODA, Julia's FBDF and QNDF and OpenModelica's DASSL and CVODE. The implicit methods are all given the exact Jacobian, sparse for the grids, as Konjugate's Stiff and BDF use exact Jacobians by default; BLAS runs on one thread, as Konjugate's serial backend does. Explicit methods are not run on Robertson or Van der Pol at μ = 1000, whose stability limits would take millions to trillions of steps. On the buck converter Konjugate locates each switch from the condition in its equation; SciPy and Julia are run both with the switch written in the right-hand side and with the switching instants given (restarted at each in SciPy, `tstops` with a callback in Julia), drawn dashed.

Solve time is kept apart from starting, loading and compiling, and both are reported: for Konjugate the engine's own integration time (its process start, project validation and planning are in the report's table), for the exported C++ the program's whole run (it has no timer of its own; its compilation, with `-std=c++20 -O2 -DNDEBUG` as the exported file's header tells its user and as the engine itself is built, is timed separately), for SciPy and Julia the call to the solver after a warm-up (Python's start and imports, Julia's start, package load and first, compiling solve of each method are in the table). The native tools are timed in one session, interleaved: for each problem, each round runs Konjugate, its C++ export, SciPy and Julia in turn, every configuration once untimed and once timed, and the rounds repeat, so that drift falls on all alike; SciPy and Julia stay running between rounds, so Julia compiles each method once.

OpenModelica has no macOS build. `npm run benchmark:openmodelica` runs it in its Linux image under Docker (pinned by digest in `comparisons/tools.mjs`), native to the processor (arm64 or x64), afterwards and on its own, translating and compiling each model once (its own timings of translation and C compilation are reported) with a symbolic Jacobian, each state's absolute tolerance being the tolerance times its `nominal` (DASSL's log confirms it; CVODE is taken to follow the same rule). Its errors are comparable with the rest; its times, inside a virtual machine with whatever background services Docker starts, are shown only as "Linux container, background services running, not timing-comparable".

What the development runs of these scripts on the computer that wrote them showed, before the published run (untimed or not under its conditions, so no numbers are given here; the published report has them):

- **Konjugate's engine against its own exported C++.** The two give the same error to every digit under Adaptive and Rodas4 (BDF's step sequence can differ by rounding, as the code export documentation says). On the larger graphs the engine's integration is about an order of magnitude slower: it evaluates every relationship's equation as interpreted MathJSON, node by node through the graph, at every stage, where the export compiles the same equations into straight-line C++. Code export is Konjugate's compiled path; the engine trades that speed for a model that can be edited and run at once.
- **Small problems.** On problems of two to five states every tool's solve takes milliseconds or less; Julia's compiled, specialised solvers are the fastest, SciPy pays Python's cost per evaluation, and Konjugate's engine sits between them in solve time, with its process start and project validation, tens of milliseconds, added to every run's wall time.
- **Stiff problems.** Konjugate's Rodas4 with its exact Jacobian is close to Julia's Rodas4 in error and work on Robertson and well ahead of SciPy's Radau; Julia's Rodas5P is ahead of both at tight tolerances.
- **Switches.** On the buck converter Konjugate locates every switch from the condition in its equation and steps to it, so its error at a given tolerance is several orders of magnitude smaller than that of a solver given the switch only in its right-hand side, which steps across the switching instants under its error control; the runs given the switching instants show how much of that the other tools recover when the modeller supplies them.
- **Failures are results.** At tight tolerances some solvers stop: OpenModelica's DASSL on Robertson at 1e-9 and 1e-10 and CVODE from 1e-7, CVODE on Van der Pol at 1e-9 and 1e-10, Julia's FBDF on Van der Pol at 1e-10; CVODE on Robertson at 1e-3 diverges. The report lists every failure with its message.
- **Parallel backends.** The thread pool costs about 50 µs per synchronisation on an Apple M1, one per step under Euler and four under Runge–Kutta 4, which made it slower than the serial backend on small graphs, where a whole serial step takes less; the partitioned backend synchronises less and did better. The published run (below) found the thread pool slower than serial at every size, not only on small graphs.

To run the comparisons alone: `node benchmarks/comparisons/compare.mjs --wait-for-quiet` (options in its header), then `node benchmarks/comparisons/report.mjs <results directory>`.

What this shows, plainly:

- **Julia is usually fastest.** Over the 18 pairs of problem and family in the comparisons where both reach 1e-6, Julia's fastest method is faster than the engine's fastest on 15. Its lead ranges from level on Robertson under BDF (QNDF's 2.02 ms, flagged noisy, against the engine's 2.04 ms) and 1.1 times on the buck converter under Adaptive, to 9.4 times on the 1,024-node grid under Adaptive.
- **Where the engine leads Julia at matched error.** On Robertson under Stiff, its Rodas4 takes 1.04 ms against 1.15 ms for Julia's Rodas5P and 2.21 ms for Julia's Rodas4. On Lorenz under BDF, it takes 1.85 ms against 2.69 ms for QNDF. On the buck converter under Stiff, its Rodas4 takes 27.7 ms against 37.1 ms for Julia's Rodas4.
- **Konjugate's engine beats SciPy's Radau on every problem,** by 4.6 times on the 1,024-node grid (23.1 ms against 106 ms) to 72 times on Robertson (1.04 ms against 74.5 ms).
- **On the grids, the engine trails SciPy under Adaptive and BDF.** On the 1,024-node grid it takes 60.2 ms against 14.6 ms for SciPy's RK45, and 49.4 ms against 16.6 ms for LSODA; on the 256-node grid, 8.78 ms against 4.51 ms and 10.4 ms against 4.24 ms. The engine evaluates every equation as interpreted MathJSON, node by node. Code export, Konjugate's compiled path, takes 20.9 ms and 41.8 ms on the 1,024-node grid's two rows.
- **Rodas5P and Tsit5.** Rodas5P is the engine's faster Stiff method on Van der Pol (2.84 ms against Rodas4's 8.12 ms), the 1,024-node grid (23.1 against 31.5 ms), the 256-node grid (4.84 against 9.24 ms) and Lorenz (1.69 against 2.38 ms); Rodas4 is faster on Robertson (1.04 against 1.24 ms), the thermal network (0.487 against 0.557 ms) and the buck converter (27.7 against 29.1 ms). Julia's Rodas5P is ahead of the engine's: 1.35 ms on Van der Pol and 15.2 ms on the 1,024-node grid. In the problems suite, Rodas5P takes Robertson to 1e11 s at a tolerance of 1e-8 in 611 steps, and Rodas4 takes 2.15 times as many. Tsit5 is close to Dormand–Prince: 0.879 against 0.976 ms on Lorenz, 8.78 against 9.43 ms on the 256-node grid, 63.3 against 60.2 ms on the 1,024-node grid and 26.0 ms for both on the buck converter.
- **Switches, like for like.** On the buck converter, with the switch written in the model for every tool, Konjugate's Rodas4 reaches 1e-6 already at a tolerance of 1e-5 (its error 2.3e-8), in 27.7 ms. Julia's Rodas4 needs a tolerance of 1e-7 and 37.1 ms, its Rodas5P 1e-8 and 50.0 ms, and SciPy's Radau 1.47 s: they step across the switching instants under their error control. Under Adaptive and BDF, Julia is ahead even so: DP5 takes 23.5 ms against the engine's 26.0 ms, and QNDF takes 25.9 ms against 44.7 ms.
- **Van der Pol at μ = 1000 under BDF.** Here Konjugate's engine and its C++ export reach 1e-6, but no SciPy or Julia multistep method does at any tolerance run. Their smallest errors are 1.9e-6 for LSODA, 3.7e-6 for SciPy's BDF, 4.0e-6 for QNDF and 1.9e-5 for FBDF.

Solve times leave out the cost of starting each tool, which the report lists separately. Konjugate's engine process adds 11.1 to 16.4 ms of median wall time beyond its solve on the small problems. On the grids it adds 110 to 250 ms, mostly reading and validating the project; planning is at most 13.2 ms of that. Python's start and SciPy's import take 0.277 s. Julia's start and package load take 1.83 s, and its first, compiling solve of a method took up to 7.45 s. The exported C++ takes 0.84 to 16.21 s to compile. It is timed as a whole program run, so its times include the program's start (its fastest run took 3.32 ms), which is why it trails the engine on the smallest problems.

## Running on release in CI

Every release runs the accuracy benchmarks: the release workflow (`.github/workflows/release.yml`) runs `node benchmarks/run.mjs --accuracy` on each of its macOS and Linux runners, in its `releaseChecks` job beside the builds, as one of its release checks (`scripts/runReleaseChecks.mjs`, with the code and FMU export fidelity checks; `npm run test:release` runs the same locally). A failing check fails the release, and the job's summary page names it. Each runner's report (`out/benchmarks/accuracy/`) is kept with the workflow run for 30 days, as `benchmark-accuracy-<os>-<arch>`, passed or failed.

Timing is deliberately not part of it: a shared CI runner's timings say more about the runner than about Konjugate.

## Adding a problem

Make a directory `benchmarks/<category>/<problem>/` with a `problem.mjs` exporting `directory`, `models` (each a function returning a project document, built with `lib/project.mjs`) and `evaluate({ engine, config })`, which returns `{ rows, checks, plots, notes }` (helpers in `lib/study.mjs`), and a `benchmark.json` with its runs, timing cases and a threshold for every check it adds. Register it in `problems.mjs`, write its models with `npm run benchmark:models`, and if it needs numerical reference data, add it to `reference/generateReferences.py`. A problem whose models are generated when it runs exports an empty `models`; one that times its own cases under `--full` (generated models, several backends) also exports `measure({ engine, config, repeats, warmupRuns })`, which returns `{ timings, rows, plots, notes }` (`scaling/graphScalability` does).

The numerical classics list their methods in `benchmark.json`, each a variant and its global step (`{ "variant": { "method": "rungeKutta4" }, "globalTimeStep": 0.0078125 }`), and name their checks after the method, so a new fixed-step method (Rodas4) is benchmarked on them by adding an entry for it and a threshold for each check it adds, once `lib/engine.mjs` passes its settings to the engine and `methodName` in `lib/study.mjs` knows its name. Adaptive, which has a tolerance rather than a step, has a section of its own in each (`adaptive` in `benchmark.json`): its global step set long enough not to bind and its samples on it, its relative tolerances from 1e-4 to 1e-10 or 1e-11 with each absolute tolerance a thousandth of it, and its work (steps taken and rejected, evaluations of the model) in every row. A decade of tolerance lengthens Lorenz's horizon by about ln 10 / λ, measured at 0.98 of it; Van der Pol's trajectory error follows the tolerance with slopes of 1.11 and 0.99; the double pendulum's energy drift over 60 s falls faster than its tolerance, slope 1.30, as a chaotic motion's does. The limit cycle's period and amplitude are measured from samples, whose spacing sets a floor a tighter tolerance does not lower, so they are checked at one tolerance. Van der Pol at μ = 1000 lists no methods yet: its entry says why, and the stiff method is its first.
