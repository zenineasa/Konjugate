# Konjugate benchmarks

Konjugate is evaluated against established numerical and engineering benchmarks, with reproducible reference comparisons, conservation checks and performance measurements. This directory holds those benchmarks: each problem's model, its reference, its configuration and its thresholds, and the runner that measures Konjugate against them and writes a report.

The benchmarks measure what Konjugate does today, including where it is weak. They are not a claim that Konjugate is accurate for every problem; they say how accurate it is for these, and why.

## What is being measured

By default Konjugate integrates every node with fixed-step Euler: explicit Euler, or semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler for a node whose `numerics.integration` is `semiImplicit` (`engine/src/executionPlan.cpp`, `compileExecutionPlan` and `integrateNode`). A semi-implicit node updates its states one after another in the order it lists them, each from contributions evaluated with the states already updated. Both methods are first order. A node may instead be integrated with Verlet (`numerics.integration: "verlet"`, [Integrators, Verlet, node by node](../docs/proposals/integrators.md#verlet-node-by-node)), Störmer–Verlet, second order and symplectic: its states, in their listed order, fall in two parts, each read only by the other (positions and their speeds), and each substep moves the first part half a step, the second a whole step and the first the other half. A node whose rates do not split so, as when a rate reads its own state, is refused in words. A run configuration may instead choose Runge–Kutta 4 (`method: "rungeKutta4"`, [Integrators](../docs/proposals/integrators.md)), the classical fourth-order method applied to the whole model at once: at each of its four stages every node is evaluated from every node's stage values, so the coupling between nodes is as accurate as the method; or Adaptive (`method: "dormandPrince54"`), Dormand–Prince 5(4) applied the same way, with error control to a relative and absolute tolerance and its steps landing on every global step; or Stiff (`method: "rodas4"`), Rodas4, a Rosenbrock method of order four, L-stable, applied the same way, its Jacobian worked out each step exactly from the equations' derivatives (or by finite differences, as it first was, when the run asks) on the structure the model's graph gives and solved with Konjugate's own LU. BDF (`method: "bdf"`), the numerical differentiation formulas of orders 1 to 5, keeps that Jacobian and factorisation from step to step, for stiff models of thousands of states.

Under Euler, each global step, every node takes its own substeps (`numerics.substepsPerGlobalStep`) and reads every other node through a snapshot frozen at the start of the global step ([Parallel execution](../docs/parallelExecution.md)). A relationship's bindings to its own target node read that node's live state; bindings to any other node read the snapshot. Coupling between nodes is therefore first order in the global step, whatever the substeps, and a semi-implicit or Verlet node's own scheme applies only to its own states, never across nodes.

The problems were chosen to suit these methods: smooth, non-stiff systems where a first-order method converges and its behaviour can be measured cleanly. Stiff problems (Robertson's chemical kinetics, Van der Pol at μ = 1000, Prothero–Robinson) are run with Stiff, and with Adaptive only over a short stretch beside it, to count the steps an explicit method needs; with fixed-step Euler or Runge–Kutta 4 they would measure only that an explicit method is unsuitable, which is known. The numerical classics (Van der Pol at μ = 1 and 10, Lorenz, the double pendulum) measure every method where it is weak as well as where it is strong: a chaotic trajectory is compared only up to the predictability horizon each method reaches, which is reported, and beyond it the system is judged by what can still be judged, its statistics and its sensitivity; Van der Pol at μ = 1000 is run with Stiff. Lorenz is split over two nodes, x and {y, z}, not three: a relationship reads only the two nodes it connects, and the equations of y and z each read both other variables (x z and x y), so three single-state nodes cannot express them today.

Each problem directory holds:

- `problem.mjs`: the model, built as an ordinary Konjugate project document; the reference; and the evaluation, which runs the model, compares it with the reference and reports rows, checks and plots. The header comment states the equations.
- `benchmark.json`: the runs (global steps, horizons, output intervals), the timing cases for `--full`, and the thresholds every check is judged against.
- The `.kjt` models, written by `node benchmarks/generateModels.mjs` through the same project encoder the app saves with. They open in Konjugate like any other project. The runner refuses to run if a committed model differs from what its `problem.mjs` builds, so a model and its reference cannot drift apart.
- `reference.json`, where the problem has numerical reference data: the data, the method, the tolerances and the versions of Python, NumPy and SciPy that made it.

A run may apply a variant to a committed model before running it, and only three kinds, recorded in every result row: the integration method of every node (`explicit`, `semiImplicit` or `verlet`), the substeps of every node, and the run's method (`rungeKutta4`, `dormandPrince54`, `rodas4` or `bdf`, with its steps per global step, its tolerance and, for Stiff, its `jacobian`, set on the run configuration the engine is given, not on the model). Robertson, Van der Pol at μ = 1000, the stiff network and Prothero–Robinson run Stiff once more with `jacobian: "finiteDifference"` beside the default exact Jacobian, and check the evaluations and error of the one against the other (and, for Prothero–Robinson, the order at the finest step, which finite differences distort). Everything else is the committed model.

### Graph scalability

`scaling/graphScalability` generates its models when it runs (`graphs.mjs` states the equations; no `.kjt` is committed): a chain of n thermal masses with both ends held at ambient, an m × m grid with its edges held at ambient, and a random sparse network (a ring plus n random paths, seeded, random conductances and capacities, no ambient). `node benchmarks/scaling/graphScalability/graphs.mjs <chain | grid | random> <nodes> <output.kjt>` writes one to open in Konjugate. The chain and grid start as a sum of three of their sine modes, so their exact motion, and each fixed-step method's exact result (a mode grows by 1 + hλ a step under Euler, by the fourth-order polynomial in hλ under Runge–Kutta 4), is known at every node at any size: Konjugate's numbers are checked against the method's exact steps (they agree to about 1e-14) and against the exact solution (the method's error). The random network is checked against the method stepped independently in the benchmark, by the matrix exponential at 100 nodes, and by its conserved total heat. Every graph runs on all three backends with the workers and partitions fixed (4 each), so the accuracy run's results do not depend on the machine; the backends must agree bit for bit. Methods are entries in its `benchmark.json`, so Dormand–Prince and Rodas4 can be added as variants: a method with no exact steps to compare with is judged against the exact solution only.

Under `--full`, the problem's `measure()` times the same families at 100, 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 nodes (each case about a million node steps, three timed runs, two at 100,000 nodes), on every backend under every method, and sweeps the worker count from 1 to 8 on a 10,000-node grid. It reports the engine process's wall time split into compiling and planning (the engine's `planningNanoseconds`), solving (its synchronization compute time) and the rest (starting, reading and validating the project, writing the result), node steps per second of solving, peak resident memory, and each parallel backend's speed-up over serial and efficiency per worker. A million nodes is not measured: the project would be about 1.45 GB of JSON, more than the project encoder can hold in one string, and the engine's validation alone would take minutes; the report says so.

### Causal reconstruction

`causal/reconstruction` measures Konjugate's causal inference (`konjugateEngine infer`, [Causal inference](../docs/causalInference.md)) on datasets whose truth is known. `datasets.mjs` generates each one from a seed: a discrete structural model stepped once per row (lag-1 linear terms, a self-lag, powers of a source, a source-target product, and, deliberately outside the model `infer` fits, a delay of two rows, an effect within one row, a hidden variable and a near-noiseless copy), or a continuous-time linear system integrated by Euler–Maruyama and sampled every 0.1, 0.5, 1 or 2 s. The CSVs and their truths are committed in `data/` (about 1.1 MB); the runner regenerates each in memory and refuses to run if a committed CSV differs. For each, the graph is hidden and `infer` runs on the CSV alone, with its default settings except the polynomial degrees or interaction terms a dataset calls for. A proposed edge is one with a lagged fit and a direction (`continuousLagged`); precision, recall and F1 are over those, a skeleton F1 also counts the `correlationOnly` pairs, direction is the share of found true edges reported in exactly the right direction, and the equation error is ‖estimated − true rates‖ / ‖true rates‖ over every true or proposed edge, in the engine's own units (rates per second of the CSV's time column). Datasets inside the model class have a floor on F1 just below its observed value; datasets showing a known limit have a band on both sides of the measure that shows it, and each says why it fails.

### Engineering systems

The problems above measure the integrators, the engine and causal inference on model problems. The engineering problems ask a different question: whether a model built the way an engineer builds one in Konjugate, as a graph of components joined by relationships, reproduces an independent reference for an interconnected system, conserves what it should, and where it falls short. They are lumped-parameter models: each node is a component or a well-mixed volume, each relationship a flow, force or signal between two of them. Spatial problems, finite-element structural analysis and computational fluid dynamics such as the NAFEMS benchmarks, are out of scope: Konjugate does not discretise partial differential equations in space, and a graph of segments (as in the heat exchanger) is the modeller's own discretisation, measured as such.

Unlike the problems above, these are not all smooth: switches, diodes, valves, clamps and step inputs are part of engineering models. Runge–Kutta 4, Adaptive and Stiff locate a switch written as a condition in an equation (docs/proposals/integrators.md, Event location) and step to it exactly; explicit Euler, node by node, does not. Each problem compares explicit Euler and Runge–Kutta 4 (and semi-implicit Euler where it matters), each at a step chosen for the problem and over a range of steps for the observed order, with the method set on the run as a variant, so a method added later (Dormand–Prince, Rodas4; [Integrators](../docs/proposals/integrators.md)) is another entry in each problem's list of methods and steps.

Each problem keeps its reference generator beside it (`generateReference.py`), with its own copy of the constants and equations, and its `reference.json` records the method, tolerances and the versions of every tool that made it. The tools are installed outside the repository and never committed:

- SciPy (`solve_ivp` DOP853 and Radau, `expm`), in the virtual environment above.
- ngspice (47, `brew install ngspice` or the platform's package) for the switching circuits.
- FMPy (0.3.32 or later, which carries CVODE for Apple Silicon) for the Reference FMUs, in its own environment: `uv venv -p 3.12 /tmp/konjugate-fmpy-venv`, then `VIRTUAL_ENV=/tmp/konjugate-fmpy-venv uv pip install fmpy numpy`.
- The Modelica Association's published MSL reference results, which `modelica/standardLibrary/generateReference.py` downloads from github.com/modelica/MAP-LIB_ReferenceResults (branch v4.1.0) and thins, recording each file's SHA-256. They are part of the Modelica Standard Library (BSD 3-Clause).
- The Reference FMUs (v0.0.42, 2-Clause BSD), committed unchanged in `fmi/referenceFmus/fmus/` with their licence; the benchmark checks their SHA-256.

What these problems show Konjugate does well: graph models of up to 26 nodes reproduce independent references to their integrators' accuracy; every balance that is linear in the states (water volume, the thermal energy of the motor and the data centre, the heat exchanger's enthalpy) is kept to rounding under both methods; Runge–Kutta 4 is fourth order on every smooth problem and, with its switches located, on every switched one but the data centre's clamps: the heat exchanger, the fluid network and all four Modelica examples among them; the discretised heat exchanger reaches its exact equilibrium to rounding, its distance from ε-NTU being the segmentation's, falling as 1/N; and the four FMI 3.0 Reference FMUs import as published and give FMPy's co-simulation of them to the bit (the difference from their model exchange is the FMUs' own fixed-step solvers, largest for the bouncing ball).

What they show it does not do, plainly:

- **Explicit Euler does not locate switches.** Under it a switch, a diode or a clamp changes at the first step that starts after the instant it should, so the switched buck converter and the rectifier converge at first order. Runge–Kutta 4, Adaptive and Stiff locate every switch written as a condition (a comparison inside a piecewise equation's condition) and step to it, so Runge–Kutta 4 is fourth order on them again: from 1.21 to 3.96 on the switched buck, 1.99 to 4.12 on the rectifier, 1.00 to 4.28 on the DC motor's step inputs and 1.00 to 4.00 on the Cauer filter's, each with its error falling by four to eight orders of magnitude at the same step.
- **Kinks are not located.** A `Min`, `Max` or `Abs` of a quantity that changes sign is continuous but not smooth, and costs an order where it turns: the data centre's cooling units clamp their demand at zero and at capacity, which keeps Runge–Kutta 4 at about 3.4 there (its time switches, the batch job and the coil failure, are located).
- **No acausal network solving or index reduction.** The Cauer filter's capacitance matrix and the drive train's rigid gear were reduced by hand to explicit equations, as a Modelica tool does automatically; the equations are the same, but the work is the modeller's.
- **Explicit Euler on undamped resonance.** The Modelica `Oscillator`, driven at resonance, is 9 % of its range off under explicit Euler at 16,000 steps per second; semi-implicit Euler, 0.3 %.
- **FMI 3 aliases are not ports.** An FMI 3 `<Alias>` (BouncingBall's `h_ft`, its height in feet) is not offered as a port of its own; the variable it names is, and is compared.

## References

Analytic references are preferred, and every problem but Lotka–Volterra and the numerical classics (Van der Pol, Lorenz, the double pendulum) has one. Linear systems (the thermal network, the RLC circuits, the coupled masses) are solved exactly with the matrix exponential of the augmented system [[A, b], [0, 0]] (`lib/linearAlgebra.mjs`, Higham's scaling and squaring with a degree 13 Padé approximant), solved about an offset near the solution (the ambient temperature) so that the exponential stays well scaled. The pendulum's exact motion is in Jacobi elliptic functions, computed by the arithmetic-geometric mean (`lib/elliptic.mjs`). The energy an RLC circuit dissipates follows exactly from its energy balance: the source's work less the energy stored.

The analytic references are themselves checked: `reference/generateReferences.py` computes each one again, independently, in Python with SciPy (`scipy.linalg.expm`, `scipy.special.ellipj`), from its own copy of the constants and equations, and each benchmark run reports how closely the two agree (relative to each quantity's magnitude, currently between 3e-15 and 1e-12).

Where there is no closed form, the reference is a numerical solution by an independent high-accuracy solver: SciPy's `solve_ivp` with the 8th-order Dormand–Prince method (DOP853) at rtol = atol = 1e-12. A numerical reference is never treated as absolute truth. Its own error is recorded where it can be measured (the drift of Lotka–Volterra's conserved quantity in the reference is about 1e-10 of the orbit's size), and it is used only where that error is far below the errors being measured. The pendulum is also solved with DOP853, as a check of the method on a problem whose answer is known.

The numerical classics have no closed form, and a chaotic reference has a further difficulty: an error in the reference grows like any other, e^(λt), so a reference is only as good as the time over which its own error stays far below what is measured. Each such problem has its own generator beside it (`generateReference.py`) and records its own error with its data. Lorenz is solved by a Taylor series method in 70-digit arithmetic, checked against itself at a lower order and precision (they agree to 6e-30 over 40 s); SciPy's DOP853 at rtol = atol = 1e-12 is solved too, and loses the trajectory (1 % of a state's range) at 33.6 s, which shows why a double-precision reference was not enough. The double pendulum is solved by DOP853 at rtol = atol = 1e-13, its own error estimated at every sample against 1e-12, and every comparison stops where that estimate reaches 1 % of the angle the comparison is judged at (17.0 s). Van der Pol is solved by Radau with the exact Jacobian at rtol = atol = 1e-12, checked against DOP853 (they agree to 4e-12 at μ = 1 and 7e-11 at μ = 10); the limit cycle's period at μ = 1, 6.663286859323169 s, is within 6e-15 of the published 6.663286859323130 s. Statistics of the Lorenz attractor are not a trajectory and need no tight trajectory: they come from 200 runs started on the attractor, each averaged over a window as long as the benchmark's own run, so the scatter across them is the scatter one run should show; their mean Lyapunov exponent, 0.9059 /s, agrees with Viswanath's 0.9056 within its standard error.

To regenerate the reference data, in a virtual environment outside the repository (the environment is not committed; the data and the scripts are):

```bash
python3 -m venv /tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/pip install numpy scipy mpmath
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/reference/generateReferences.py
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/oscillators/vanDerPol/generateReference.py
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/chaos/lorenz/generateReference.py
/tmp/konjugate-benchmarks-venv/bin/python benchmarks/mechanical/doublePendulum/generateReference.py
```

The Lorenz generator takes a few minutes (the 70-digit Taylor series) and Van der Pol's several (Radau at μ = 1000); every reference.json records the versions of Python, NumPy, SciPy and, for Lorenz, mpmath that made it.

## Metrics

Every comparison is made at the run's output samples, which are the same for every step size in a study.

- **Max abs error**: the largest |Konjugate - reference| over every compared state and sample, in the states' own units.
- **RMSE**: the root mean square of the same differences.
- **Relative error**: for each state, its largest absolute error divided by the reference's range over the run (max - min); the largest over the states. The range is used rather than the value, so a temperature near 300 K or a current passing through zero is judged against how much it actually moves.
- **Conservation error**: the problem's conserved or balanced quantity, from Konjugate's own states. For the thermal network, the energy balance Σ C ΔT against heat supplied less heat lost, relative to heat supplied. For the RLC circuits, the source's work V q against energy stored plus energy dissipated, relative to the source's work. For the oscillator and pendulum, the largest drift of the energy relative to its initial value (for the pendulum, relative to the energy above the bottom of the swing, E(0) + g L). For Lotka–Volterra, the largest drift of V = δx - γ ln x + βy - α ln y relative to V(0) - V*, the orbit's level above the equilibrium.
- **Steady-state error**: the largest difference between the final state of a long run and the exact equilibrium A x* = -b.
- **Observed order**: p in error ≈ C hᵖ, from each halving of the step (log₂ of the error ratio) and as the least-squares slope of log error against log h. Euler's methods should show p ≈ 1, Verlet p ≈ 2 (judged over Euler's step sizes, on the oscillator and the pendulum only: in the thermal network and the RLC circuits a state's rate reads that state itself, a loss, which Verlet refuses), Runge–Kutta 4 p ≈ 4, Dormand–Prince 5(4) with its error control off p ≈ 5 (a run configuration turns it off by giving it `stepsPerGlobalStep`). Runge–Kutta 4 is judged over step sizes of its own (`rungeKutta4GlobalSteps` in `verification/convergenceOrder/benchmark.json`), chosen where its error is well above rounding and its step inside its stability region: at Euler's steps its error would reach rounding within a halving or two. Dormand–Prince 5(4) likewise (`dormandPrince54GlobalSteps`); it is not judged on the thermal network, whose error passes from steps too long to show the order straight to the reference's rounding.
- **Tolerance proportionality** (Adaptive): over relative tolerances from 1e-3 to 1e-10, each absolute tolerance a thousandth of it, with a global step a sixteenth of the run so the global step does not bind, the least-squares slope of log error against log tolerance, and the largest error over the tolerance. Dormand–Prince's slope is a little under 1, its error estimate being of order four. Each run's work (steps taken and rejected, evaluations of the model) is in its row, and each problem has a work–precision plot of every method's contributions evaluated against the error it reached.
- **Coupling error**: for the split model, its difference from the one-node model run at the same effective step (the global step divided by the substeps), relative to range.
- **Predictability horizon**: for a chaotic problem, the first sample time at which the error exceeds a stated tolerance (for Lorenz 1 % of a state's range, for the double pendulum 0.1 rad in either angle). An error grows like e^(λt), so halving the step of a method of order p gains only about p ln 2 / λ seconds of horizon.
- **Statistical score**: for a long-run property of the Lorenz attractor (the mean and standard deviation of z, the root mean square of x, the largest Lyapunov exponent), |Konjugate - reference| in units of the scatter of one run as long as Konjugate's across the reference ensemble. One run cannot be expected to land closer than about 1 to 2; a method whose score stays well above that has moved the attractor.
- **Limit cycle**: the Van der Pol cycle's period, from upward zero crossings of x, and its amplitude, the largest x, both found between samples by cubic Hermite interpolation with the exact slopes, after a long transient, relative to the reference's.
- **Work**: the global steps, the node substeps executed and the contributions evaluated, summed over nodes, as the engine reports them (`execution.nodeMetrics`).

## Thresholds

Every check is judged against a threshold in its problem's `benchmark.json`, so a change in Konjugate's numbers shows up as a failing check rather than a silent difference. Accuracy is deterministic: the same engine on the same platform gives the same numbers, and across platforms they differ only by rounding. The thresholds are set as follows:

- An error at a configured step has a ceiling about 25 % above its observed value: a regression guard, not a claim that the error is small.
- A known weakness of explicit Euler (the growth of an undamped oscillator's energy, the drift of Lotka–Volterra's conserved quantity) has a band on both sides of its observed value, so a change to the integrator that alters it in either direction is noticed and the benchmark updated knowingly.
- Quantities that should be at rounding level (an energy balance that explicit Euler keeps exactly, a steady state it reaches exactly, the agreement of two exact references) have absolute limits a few orders above rounding.
- Identities (a split model with one substep against one node, semi-implicit against explicit integration for one-state nodes, and under Runge–Kutta 4 the split model against one node at any step, under Adaptive at any tolerance) must hold exactly, or to 1e-12.
- A predictability horizon, or a statistical score of a method known to move the attractor, has a band on both sides of its observed value; a score of Runge–Kutta 4 at a step where it does not move the attractor must be at most 3.
- Euler's observed orders must lie within 0.95 to 1.10 (fitted over all six steps) and 0.97 to 1.03 (from the finest halving); Verlet's within 1.9 to 2.1, Runge–Kutta 4's within 3.9 to 4.1 and Dormand–Prince 5(4)'s at fixed steps within 4.9 to 5.1, both fitted and from the finest halving. Adaptive's slope of error against tolerance must lie within 0.8 to 1.15, and its error within 20 times its relative tolerance.

When a deliberate change to the engine moves a result, the threshold is updated with the change, and the reason recorded in its commit.

## Running

```bash
npm run benchmark:accuracy   # builds the engine, then: node benchmarks/run.mjs --accuracy
npm run benchmark:full       # builds the engine, then: node benchmarks/run.mjs --full
npm run benchmark:models     # writes the .kjt models from the problems
```

`--accuracy` runs every problem, deterministically and with no timing, and exits with status 1 if any check fails. It takes about forty seconds and writes to `out/benchmarks/accuracy/`. `--full` runs the same accuracy checks and then the timing cases, and writes to `benchmarks/results/<version>/<machine>/`. Other options: `--engine <path>` (or `KONJUGATE_ENGINE`) to use a particular engine build, `--output <dir>`, `--only <text>` to run the problems whose ID contains the text, and for `--full`, `--repeats <n>` (default 7), `--warmup <n>` (default 1) and `--no-caffeinate`.

Each run writes:

- `report.html`: one self-contained page (no network, no external files) with every problem's plots of Konjugate against its reference, its checks and its result rows, and for `--full` the timings and the machine. Each plot has a data table, and its full data is in `plots/`.
- `results.csv`: every result row, with the columns described under Metrics.
- `checks.csv`: every check, its value, its threshold and whether it passed.
- `summary.json`: the run (versions, commit, mode), the totals, every problem's rows and checks, and for `--full` the machine and timings.
- `plots/<id>.svg` and `plots/<id>.csv`: each plot on its own, and its data.
- `timing.csv` (`--full` only): each timing case's runs, median and spread.

## Timing

Timing is measured only by `--full`, and only means anything on the machine that measured it. It depends on the processor, its power state and thermal state, the operating system's scheduling and whatever else is running, none of which travel with the code. So `--full` records the machine with its results (model, processor and core counts, memory, operating system, power source, battery charge and Low Power Mode where the platform reports them, and the load average at the start; never its host name, user or serial number), and its results are kept per version and per machine under `benchmarks/results/`. Comparing timings across machines, or across versions measured on different machines, compares the machines as much as Konjugate.

For each timing case, the runner makes untimed warm-up runs, then the timed runs one at a time, and reports the median wall time with its minimum, maximum and median absolute deviation, the median of the engine's own integration time (`execution.synchronizationComputeNanoseconds`), the node substeps integrated per second, and the engine process's peak resident memory (from `/usr/bin/time` on macOS and Linux). Wall time covers the whole engine process: starting, reading the model, integrating and writing the result.

Timing runs need a quiet machine. Before timing, the runner looks for Konjugate's interaction test runners, Konjugate's own Electron (the development build or the installed app) and test-driven Chrome, and warns if any is running, as it does when the computer is on battery or in Low Power Mode; the warning is kept in the report. On macOS it runs under `caffeinate -ims`, so the computer does not sleep or idle-throttle during the run.

## Running on release in CI

Every release runs the accuracy benchmarks: the release workflow (`.github/workflows/release.yml`) runs `node benchmarks/run.mjs --accuracy` on each of its macOS and Linux runners, in its `releaseChecks` job beside the builds, as one of its release checks (`scripts/runReleaseChecks.mjs`, with the code and FMU export fidelity checks; `npm run test:release` runs the same locally). A failing check fails the release, and the job's summary page names it. Each runner's report (`out/benchmarks/accuracy/`) is kept with the workflow run for 30 days, as `benchmark-accuracy-<os>-<arch>`, passed or failed.

Timing is deliberately not part of it: a shared CI runner's timings say more about the runner than about Konjugate.

## Adding a problem

Make a directory `benchmarks/<category>/<problem>/` with a `problem.mjs` exporting `directory`, `models` (each a function returning a project document, built with `lib/project.mjs`) and `evaluate({ engine, config })`, which returns `{ rows, checks, plots, notes }` (helpers in `lib/study.mjs`), and a `benchmark.json` with its runs, timing cases and a threshold for every check it adds. Register it in `problems.mjs`, write its models with `npm run benchmark:models`, and if it needs numerical reference data, add it to `reference/generateReferences.py`. A problem whose models are generated when it runs exports an empty `models`; one that times its own cases under `--full` (generated models, several backends) also exports `measure({ engine, config, repeats, warmupRuns })`, which returns `{ timings, rows, plots, notes }` (`scaling/graphScalability` does).

The numerical classics list their methods in `benchmark.json`, each a variant and its global step (`{ "variant": { "method": "rungeKutta4" }, "globalTimeStep": 0.0078125 }`), and name their checks after the method, so a new fixed-step method (Rodas4) is benchmarked on them by adding an entry for it and a threshold for each check it adds, once `lib/engine.mjs` passes its settings to the engine and `methodName` in `lib/study.mjs` knows its name. Adaptive, which has a tolerance rather than a step, has a section of its own in each (`adaptive` in `benchmark.json`): its global step set long enough not to bind and its samples on it, its relative tolerances from 1e-4 to 1e-10 or 1e-11 with each absolute tolerance a thousandth of it, and its work (steps taken and rejected, evaluations of the model) in every row. A decade of tolerance lengthens Lorenz's horizon by about ln 10 / λ, measured at 0.98 of it; Van der Pol's trajectory error follows the tolerance with slopes of 1.11 and 0.99; the double pendulum's energy drift over 60 s falls faster than its tolerance, slope 1.30, as a chaotic motion's does. The limit cycle's period and amplitude are measured from samples, whose spacing sets a floor a tighter tolerance does not lower, so they are checked at one tolerance. Van der Pol at μ = 1000 lists no methods yet: its entry says why, and the stiff method is its first.
